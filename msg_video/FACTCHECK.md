## Claim-by-claim

### Cold open (s01)

**1. Blaylock clips: real words, fair framing**: ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: Both quotes come from a Whisper transcript of the archived lecture and match his long-stated position that dietary MSG is a harmful "excitotoxin" (his book *Excitotoxins: The Taste That Kills*). That position is not supported by regulators or scientific consensus, so showing his words and then the evidence is fair comment.
- Nuance:
  - **Clip A was trimmed.** The source says "...the food manufacturers know this, that's why they put so much MSG in food because it makes you hungry...". The clip starts at "They put", so "they" (food manufacturers) has no antecedent. The meaning is kept, which is acceptable.
  - **Clip A2 is true on its own terms.** "Excitotoxicity destroys brain cells" is accurate neuroscience: excitotoxicity is a real mechanism, for example in stroke. His contested claim is that *eating MSG* causes it. As cut, viewers could think the Reel is debunking excitotoxicity itself. The narration never says excitotoxicity is false, so this is not an error, but see optional fix O2.
  - **The hunger claim is never addressed.** The Reel answers the brain claim (gut, blood-brain barrier) but not "makes you hungry". The evidence there is mixed. Some studies find MSG in soup increases satiety (Masic & Yeomans 2014).
  - **The quotes come from machine transcripts.** CANDIDATES.md says a human ear check is mandatory, because two Whisper models disagreed on wording. The script header calls the quotes "verified against a transcript", but that transcript is machine-made. Nothing on file shows the ear check was done.
- Defamation risk: low. The Reel attributes only his own recorded words and responds with evidence. It does not call him a liar or a quack. Keep it that way, with no on-screen mockery of him personally.
- Sources:
  - https://archive.org/details/youtube-Y0kvaulUIfc
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Blaylock
  - Masic U, Yeomans MR (2014), Am J Clin Nutr 100(2):532-538, MSG and satiety (citation; link not verified in this session)

**2. Credit line "Neurosurgeon, lecture on MSG, 2012"**: OVERSTATED / partly WRONG
- Evidence: Blaylock is a *retired* neurosurgeon. The lecture is titled "Nutrition & Behavior: Dangers of Aspartame & MSG". **2012 is only the YouTube upload date (2012-10-04).** The archive metadata gives no venue or date for the lecture itself, and it may be older. "Neurosurgeon" alone implies he is practising now, and "2012" states an unverified lecture date as fact. HANDOFF.md also asks that a credit name the outlet. Here the honest outlet is "lecture video, posted 2012".
- Sources:
  - https://archive.org/details/youtube-Y0kvaulUIfc (metadata: uploader ZeroEightyFour, 2012-10-04, no lecture date)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Blaylock ("Neurosurgeon (retired), writer, lecturer")

**3. "For fifty years, everyone has been scared of MSG"** (label "50 years"): OVERSTATED (mild)
- Evidence: 1968 to 2026 is 58 years, so "fifty" undercounts. "Everyone" is hyperbole: surveys find a large minority of people avoid MSG, not everyone.
- Sources:
  - https://hekint.org/2021/06/04/a-bad-taste-in-the-mouth-over-fifty-years-of-doubt-about-msg/
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/shm/hkn098

**4. "It all started with one letter, in 1968"**: ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: Historians date the public scare to Kwok's April 1968 *NEJM* letter. Lab concern existed earlier: Lucas & Newhouse reported MSG retinal damage in mice in 1957. Olney's 1969 work then amplified the scare.
- Sources:
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/shm/hkn098 (Mosby 2009)
  - https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-rotten-science-behind-the-msg-scare/

### How the rumour started (s06)

**5. "So how did a seaweed seasoning become a villain?"**: ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: Ikeda isolated glutamate from kombu seaweed. Commercial Ajinomoto MSG was made from wheat protein, and today it is made by bacterial fermentation of starch or sugar, so "seaweed seasoning" describes its origin story, not its production. This is acceptable as shorthand.
- Source: https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-science-of-satisfaction/

**6. "In 1968, a doctor wrote to a medical journal: after Chinese food, he felt numb and weak."**: ACCURATE
- Evidence: Robert Ho Man Kwok, MD, wrote in *NEJM* 278:796 (4 April 1968). He described numbness at the back of the neck radiating to the arms and back, general weakness and palpitations, starting soon after eating in Chinese restaurants, "especially" northern Chinese ones.
- Nuance: in 2019, surgeon Howard Steel claimed he wrote the letter as a prank. *This American Life* reported that Kwok was a real physician and that his family said he wrote it. Either way, "a doctor wrote" holds.
- Sources:
  - https://www.thisamericanlife.org/668/transcript
  - https://news.colgate.edu/magazine/2019/02/06/the-strange-case-of-dr-ho-man-kwok
  - https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-rotten-science-behind-the-msg-scare/

**7. "Wine? Salt? MSG? He was guessing."** (label "Guessing"): ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: Kwok listed three possible causes: cooking wine, MSG and high sodium. He tested none of them and invited colleagues to investigate. "Guessing" is a fair, slightly flip summary of "speculating".
- Sources:
  - https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-rotten-science-behind-the-msg-scare/
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/shm/hkn098

**8. "But the journal gave it a catchy headline: Chinese Restaurant Syndrome."** (label "Chinese-Restaurant Syndrome"): ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: The letter ran under the heading "Chinese-Restaurant Syndrome". Reporting (Axios 2024, Science History Institute) says Kwok did not use that phrase in the body, so the heading is attributed to the journal. NEJM has never publicly confirmed who chose it. The journal then ran follow-up letters and a mocking editorial that spread the name.
- The hyphenated label matches the original heading.
- Sources:
  - https://www.axios.com/local/san-francisco/2024/08/29/chinatown-msg-chinese-restaurant-syndrome-nejm
  - https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/disappearing-pod/mass-psychosis-in-food-science/

**9. "Then mice injected with huge doses got brain damage."** (label "Huge doses"): ACCURATE
- Evidence: Olney (1969, *Science* 164:719) gave newborn mice subcutaneous MSG injections. This caused acute neuronal necrosis, including in the hypothalamus, followed by stunting and obesity. The doses were grams per kilogram of body weight, far beyond what people eat.
- Sources:
  - https://doi.org/10.1126/science.164.3880.719
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Olney

**10. "But injecting a baby mouse is nothing like eating dinner."**: ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: The point stands on route, age and dose. However, Olney & Ho (1970) also produced lesions with *oral* force-fed doses (about 3 g/kg) in infant mice. The real reason it is "nothing like dinner" is the enormous dose, the immature animal and the lack of food. Oral MSG with food did not produce lesions, and infant monkeys fed oral MSG showed none.
- Sources:
  - https://www.nature.com/articles/227609b0 (Olney & Ho 1970, oral dosing)
  - https://karger.com/anm/article/73/Suppl.%205/43/42468/Monosodium-Glutamate-in-the-Diet-Does-Not-Raise

**11. "Nobody panicked about parmesan. The fear stuck to one cuisine, and the name said it out loud."**: ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: Mosby (2009) documents that the syndrome became tied to Chinese cooking, even though American food manufacturers used MSG widely. Parmesan and tomatoes are rich in free glutamate and drew no alarm.
- Nuance: the fear did spread beyond one cuisine. US makers pulled MSG from baby food in 1969, and "No MSG" labels later appeared on Western snacks. "Stuck to" (rather than "was limited to") is defensible.
- Sources:
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/shm/hkn098
  - https://www.yorku.ca/yfile/?p=15567

### Discovery (s03) and glutamate everywhere (s04)

**12. 1908, Kikunae Ikeda, seaweed soup, "not sweet, sour, salty or bitter", umami, turned into MSG** (labels "1908", "Seaweed", "Umami", "MSG"): ACCURATE
- Evidence:
  - Ikeda, a chemistry professor at Tokyo Imperial University, traced the taste of kombu dashi (in a boiled-tofu dish) to glutamate in 1908, coined "umami" and patented a glutamate seasoning that year. AJI-NO-MOTO launched in 1909.
  - Umami was later confirmed as a basic taste with its own receptor (T1R1/T1R3).
- Sources:
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/chemse/27.9.847 (Ikeda 1909, translated 2002)
  - https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-science-of-satisfaction/
  - https://www.ajinomoto.co.jp/company/en/amino/aminosan/kaisetsu05.html

**13. "Glutamate is everywhere: tomatoes, parmesan, even breast milk."**: ACCURATE
- Evidence: Free glutamate per 100 g is about 1,200 mg in parmesan and about 140–250 mg in fresh tomato. Human milk has about 15–30 mg/100 ml, and glutamate is its most abundant free amino acid (Agostoni 2000).
- Sources:
  - https://www.dfg.de/resource/blob/169090/56fb70f1c9bf591806fad4c45ef53098/sklm-glutamat-2005-en-data.pdf
  - https://www.andeal.org/vault/2440/web/Umami%20in%20Foods%20White%20Paper.pdf
  - Agostoni C et al. (2000), J Pediatr Gastroenterol Nutr 31:508-512 (as cited in SCRIPT.md)

**14. "Glutamate from a tomato and from MSG is the exact same molecule."** (label "Same molecule"): ACCURATE
- Evidence: The FDA states that glutamate in MSG is chemically indistinguishable from glutamate in food proteins and is metabolized the same way. Both are L-glutamate.
- Source: https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/questions-and-answers-monosodium-glutamate-msg

### What happens when you eat it (s05)

**15. "The cells lining your gut burn glutamate as fuel. Around ninety-five percent never even reaches your blood."** (label "95%"): ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: Reeds et al. (2000) found that 95% of dietary glutamate reaching the gut lining was metabolized on first pass, and glutamate was the largest single fuel for the gut. That figure comes from piglets. In adult humans, about 96% is removed on first pass by the splanchnic bed (gut plus liver).
- Nuance:
  - Only about half is literally burned to CO2. The rest becomes other amino acids and glutathione.
  - "Never reaches your blood" means it never reaches the systemic circulation. A little enters portal blood.
- Sources:
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/130.4.978S (Reeds 2000)
  - https://vivo.weill.cornell.edu/display/pubid7653544 (human splanchnic study, 1995)
  - https://karger.com/anm/article/73/Suppl.%205/15/42462/The-Roles-of-Dietary-Glutamate-in-the-Intestine

**16. "The rest hits the blood-brain barrier, one of the best-guarded borders in your body."** (label "Blood-brain barrier"): ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: Hawkins (2009) shows the blood-brain barrier is essentially impermeable to glutamate, even at high blood levels, and actively pumps it out of the brain. The exceptions are the small circumventricular organs.
- Nuance:
  - The circumventricular organs include the arcuate area where Olney's lesions occurred.
  - Most of "the rest" goes to other tissues rather than "hitting" the barrier.
- Sources:
  - https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.2009.27462BB
  - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5192417/

### Putting MSG to the test (s07)

**17. "In double-blind studies, people who said MSG made them sick reacted just as often to a placebo."** (labels "Double-blind", "Placebo"): **OVERSTATED**
- Evidence: In the study the script cites (Geha 2000, 130 self-reported reactors, 5 g MSG with no food), **38.5% reacted only to MSG versus 13.1% only to placebo.** So not "just as often". The responses were inconsistent on re-challenge and did not appear when MSG was taken with food. The FDA's own wording is that scientists "have not been able to consistently trigger reactions".
- The "nearly equal" bars in s07.02 inherit the same overstatement.
- Sources:
  - https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10736382/
  - https://www.em-consulte.com/article/573858/multicenter-double-blind-placebo-controlled-multip
  - https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/questions-and-answers-monosodium-glutamate-msg

**18. "The FDA calls it generally recognized as safe. Just like salt."** (label "GRAS"): ACCURATE
- Evidence: The FDA considers added MSG GRAS. 21 CFR 182.1(a) names "salt, pepper, vinegar, baking powder, and monosodium glutamate" together as safe for their intended use.
- Nuance: EFSA (2017) set an ADI of 30 mg/kg/day and noted that some high consumers exceed it. This does not contradict the FDA line, but "safe" is still dose-dependent.
- Sources:
  - https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/182.1
  - https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/questions-and-answers-monosodium-glutamate-msg
  - https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/4910

### Plot twist (s08)

**19. "It has a third of the sodium of table salt."** (label "1/3 sodium"): ACCURATE
- Evidence: MSG is about 12.3 g sodium per 100 g versus about 39.3 g for salt, a ratio of 0.31.
- Sources:
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7540316
  - https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2019/11/08/Study-looks-at-reducing-sodium-intake-through-MSG-substitution-in-salty-food-categories/

**20. "Same flavor, less sodium."**: **WRONG**
- Evidence: MSG tastes umami, not salty, so it is not "the same flavor" as salt and cannot replace it one-for-one. Studies show that swapping *part* of the salt for MSG can cut sodium (about 25–40% in some dishes, about 3–8% at population level) while keeping dishes as well liked.
- The s08.01 scale showing the two shakers "level" is fine as a picture, but the spoken "same flavor" is false.
- Sources:
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7540316
  - https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2019/11/08/Study-looks-at-reducing-sodium-intake-through-MSG-substitution-in-salty-food-categories/

### Outro (s09)

**21. "So, is MSG bad for you? Not really."** (label "Not really"): ACCURATE WITH NUANCE
- Evidence: The FDA, JECFA (ADI "not specified") and the FASEB 1995 review consider MSG safe at normal intakes. FASEB noted mild, transient symptoms in some sensitive people who took 3 g or more without food. "Not really" (rather than "No") correctly leaves that room.
- Sources:
  - https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/questions-and-answers-monosodium-glutamate-msg
  - https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/4910

**22. "A guess, a catchy name, and a little prejudice outlived decades of science."**: ACCURATE WITH NUANCE (opinion)
- Evidence: This is a fair editorial summary of the history (Mosby 2009; Axios 2024 on NEJM's headline). It is clearly opinion, and it targets a label, not a person.
- Sources:
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/shm/hkn098
  - https://www.axios.com/local/san-francisco/2024/08/29/chinatown-msg-chinese-restaurant-syndrome-nejm
